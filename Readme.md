Life without parole for juveniles 

Topic: Life Sentencing with no chance of parole Stunts Juvenile development 

Introduction 

On September 22, 2006, two highschoolers aged 16: Brian Draper and Torey Adamcik planned and murdered their classmate, Cassie Jo Stoddart in Idaho (Rofé). For their actions, they have been sentenced to life with no chance of parole. Paula Cooper committed a murder at age 15; she was sentenced to life in prison at 16, and she earned a college degree while behind bars and released in 2013 on parole after serving 27 years. A Juvenile's development does not stop when the crime occurs, therefore a punishment with no possibility of parole judges them permanently in their period of psychological development and capacity for change is continuing. 

Joshua Rofe's documentary examines the lives of several prisoners' serving life without parole who committed their crimes while in their adolescence. It sets the current lives of these prisoners' side by side with the crimes they committed.  

Because adolescence is a period of continued development mentally, minors who commit murder should not be given life without parole; they should receive serious punishment while leaving room for later individualized review based on development and behavior. 

Analysis 

Research on adolescent development provides the strongest arguments on why juveniles should be treated differently from adults. Steinberg and Scott study juvenile criminal responsibility through developmental psychology in their article "Less Guilty by Reason of Adolescence". Rather than claiming adolescents cannot tell between right or wrong, the authors examine the areas in which adolescents are different from adults, for example their susceptibility to peer pressure, thought of risk and self-management. Their work distinguishes between developmental issues from being able to make decisions under pressure. Adolescents can likely reason, but their decisions are influenced by immediate circumstances. Steinberg and Scott also reason that adolescents become more future oriented as they mature throughout their period of adolescence. These differences do not excuse murderers but do make a defense on why crime committed during adolescence should not be treated on a similar level as ones committed by adults.  

The Consequence of treating juvenile crime as a permanent scar can also be seen in butler's research of juvenile life at prison without parole. Steinberg and Scott discuss that adolescents are more vulnerable to impulsive behavior and less capable of considering long term consequences than adults. 

Butler interviewed eleven men sentenced to life in prison without parole for crimes committed as juveniles.  Adolescence is a period where judgement and control continue to mature. His research moves the discussion beyond just the moment of the crime. The crime committed doesn't only have an effect on teenagers but the adults they become. Years of education, reflection, and maturity can change their perspective on their actions. Yet a sentence without parole provides no opportunity for the chance to have such a development. 

In the documentary, Lot for life the change between adolescents becomes apparent through Brian Draper seen between his sixteen-year-old self and the current age in the documentary which happens to be twenty-one years. In the documentary, Brain says, " I owe a tremendous debt to Cassie Stoddart. So then I have to wonder, what does that say about me and where I am in my life?". This shows the directed blame towards his own actions and regret which shows clear signs of development even without receiving dedicated psychological help sessions. 

Discussion 

To achieve a morally balanced approach without losing the consequences for their actions, punishments should be considered at the juvenile level while allowing for rehabilitation and evaluation later in life. Instead of sentencing every juvenile who committed a murder with a life sentence, a long period of prison time followed by individualized review based on the servarity of the crime, behavior after being incarcerated and before, educational achievement, rehabilitation efforts etc should be considered. This would allow punishment to remain serious while still allowing a chance at life for the undeveloped youth.  

The legal system has overtime come to recognize that juvenile development matters when deciding on the punishment.  

Conclusion 

The question of how Juvenile murderers should be looked at cannot be considered by looking at the crime alone. The age and development conditions of the person who committed the crime must be considered. Juveniles, unlike adults, have a high capacity to change psychologically as much as physically. The crime committed cannot be erased and the punishment should not disappear, but neither should the chance for change. A person should be held accountable for their actions without assuming the worst thing they have done is all they will ever become, as said by Bryan Stevenson.
